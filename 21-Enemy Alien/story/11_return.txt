# PART TEN - 1945: the return, and the epilogue

== return_1945
## Part Ten — The Lutetia
The war does not end when Paris is liberated. It goes on for another eight months, somewhere else, to the east, and Paris is cold and hungry through another winter, the winter of 1944, with no coal again and the bread ration cut again, and the Americans everywhere in the streets with their chewing gum and their cigarettes, which are money now instead of German marks.

? felix_alive : In December a letter comes for you, forwarded three times, with American stamps. It is from Felix. He got out. Varian Fry got him a visa in the spring of 1941; he crossed the Pyrenees on foot with his violin on his back, and sailed from Lisbon to New York. He plays in the orchestra of a cinema in Brooklyn, between the newsreel and the feature. He is married, to a girl from Cleveland who plays the harp. "I owe you a wedding," he writes. "Josette's. I'm still coming. Tell her to choose a husband who likes Haydn." He writes nothing about his mother in Vienna. At the bottom, in a different ink, as if he added it later, he writes: "Theresienstadt, they think. Then nobody knows."

? sarah_safe | moise_safe : In March, Annette Monod brings the children back from the plateau.
? sarah_safe : Sarah is thirteen and a half, and taller than Madeleine, and speaks French with the accent of the Haute-Loire, and has a Protestant hymn book that she reads in bed.
? moise_safe : Moïse is five and doesn't remember Paris, or the flat, or the Lewins. He remembers the calf. He asks when he can go back to the calf.

In April the camps in Germany begin to be opened.

The photographs come first, in the newspapers, from Buchenwald, from Bergen-Belsen, from Dachau. Nobody believes them. Then they have to.

Then the trains start to come back, and the lorries, and the aeroplanes, with the deportees. They are brought to the Hotel Lutetia, on the boulevard Raspail, a big Art Nouveau hotel on the left bank that was the headquarters of German military intelligence for four years, and which is now a reception centre. Deportees are registered, deloused, examined, fed, given a little money and a ration card and a bed. Their names are read out over loudspeakers. Their names are posted on lists in the hall.

And outside the Lutetia, on the boulevard, every day, from morning to night, there is a crowd of people holding up photographs.

Some of them are recognised, and there is screaming, and people fall into each other's arms on the pavement and the crowd closes round them. Most of them are not.

The crowd holds up the photographs. Have you seen him? Have you seen her? Auschwitz? Buchenwald? Ravensbrück? Do you know? Did you see?

On the fifth day a woman stops in front of your sign.

She is perhaps forty, perhaps seventy, there is no way of knowing. She has a headscarf over her shaved head. On her left forearm, below the sleeve of a Red Cross coat, there is a blue number, tattooed.

She reads the sign. She reads it very slowly, as if reading is something she has to remember how to do.

"Pithiviers," she says. "The convoy from Pithiviers. In August 1942." Her French has a Polish accent like Rivka's. "I was on the convoy of the thirty-first of July. From Pithiviers. With the mothers. They had taken our children from us in the camp. In Auschwitz they made a selection on the platform. Right, left. Young women who could work, to the right. To the camp. Everybody else, to the left. To the showers, they said." She looks at the sign. "Nobody who went to the left came back. They were dead in an hour. The same day. The children who came after, in August, without us, they all went left. All of them. Every child. The same day."

? sarah_safe : Sarah is standing beside you with the sign. She heard it. You watch her hear it. She doesn't move.

The woman looks at you. "I'm sorry," she says. "Somebody should tell you. Nobody tells anybody. You stand here for weeks. I'm sorry. Don't stand here any more."

She walks away down the boulevard Raspail, very slowly.

Of the thirteen thousand people arrested in the Vel d'Hiv roundup, fewer than a hundred come back. Of the four thousand children, none.

== lucien_return
## Lucien
Lucien comes back on the twenty-ninth of April.

You don't recognise him. He recognises you. A skeleton in a striped jacket and an American army greatcoat, with a shaved head and no teeth at the front, comes out of the doors of the Lutetia leaning on a nurse and stops dead on the steps and says "Emil," in a voice like paper tearing, and you look and look and it is Lucien, Lucien who weighed a hundred kilos, who now weighs forty-one.

Buchenwald. Then a subcamp called Dora, where they built the V2 rockets in tunnels under a mountain, and men died at the rate of a hundred a day. Then a death march in April, west, ahead of the Russians, and an open railway wagon, and the Americans.

You take him home. Madeleine puts him in your bed. He sleeps for two days. When he wakes up he asks for wine and Madeleine gives him milk and he drinks it and cries.

On the fourth day, sitting up in the bed with his hands round a bowl of soup, he says, "I know you were in the rue des Saussaies. Stoltenberg told me. He liked to tell us who'd been in. He said you were very brave." He smiles with no front teeth. "Were you?"

You gave them his name. You said it to the floor of a cell. They already had it. They took him two days before. It made no difference. It made all the difference.

You tell him. All of it. The bath. The third day. Stoltenberg saying, You'd only be confirming. You saying it to the floor. The print shop with the paper seal. The dairy woman spitting.

He listens with the bowl of soup going cold in his hands. When you have finished, he puts the bowl down on the bedside table, very carefully, with both hands, because his hands shake.

"Emil," he says. "In Buchenwald, there was a block elder, a German Communist, a comrade, been in the camps since 1933. A hero. He saved hundreds of men. He put their names on the lists for the good work details instead of the transports. And to do that he had to put other names on the transports, to make up the numbers. Every week. He chose. Every week he chose who would die so that someone else could live." He looks at you. "Everybody gave something. Everybody. The only people who didn't give anything are the people who weren't asked."

He holds out his hand. It is a bundle of sticks. You take it.

"You stupid Boche," he says. "You confirmed it. I'd already given them my own name, the first day, when they asked me what it was."

## The Eighth of May
On the eighth of May 1945, at three o'clock in the afternoon, the bells ring again, all over Paris, and General de Gaulle on the radio says that the war is won.

There are crowds on the Champs-Élysées again, and on the place de la Concorde, and dancing in every street. There are American soldiers on the roofs of jeeps, kissing girls. There is a man in the rue de la Roquette playing an accordion on a chair in the middle of the street, and everybody in the building comes down to dance in the courtyard, around the dry fountain.

You do not dance. You sit on the steps of number 64 with your hand that does not close, and you watch.

? moise_safe : Moïse is asleep on your lap, five years old, heavy and warm.
? pierrot : Pierrot is on the roof of the workshop, with two other boys, throwing paper planes into the courtyard. He is not frightened of planes any more.
? parents_safe : Isaac dances with Rivka. Very slowly, in the middle of the courtyard, to a waltz from the accordion, as if they were twenty years old in Łódź.

? pujol_hostile : Madame Pujol does not come out of her lodge. They say she is going to live with a niece in Clermont-Ferrand.

Madeleine comes and sits beside you on the step and puts her head on your shoulder.

"What are you thinking about?" she says.

You are thinking about a ravine with birch trees. About a pram in a church. About a girl in a pinafore on the stairs. About a woman in a yellow dress lying in the road with her arms out. About a man in a cardigan from Eimsbüttel with a pot of geraniums. About Lindner on a bench in the sun, taking a little rest. About your brother.

? oradour_promise : About a promise you made to an old man on a bicycle, on the wall of a churchyard, to tell them, in Germany, in German.

"Nothing," you say. "Everything. I don't know."

"Me neither," says Madeleine. "Let's just sit."

So you sit.

== epilogue
You live a long time. Longer than you expected; longer, some days, than you would have chosen.

You go back to work. You learn to sew with your left hand, and with the two good fingers of your right. It takes you two years. Your work is never quite as fine again. You do not tell your customers why, and they do not ask. In 1949 you become a French citizen, at last, in a ceremony at the Prefecture of Police, where the clerk who used to sigh at you shakes your hand, and does not sigh.

? oradour_promise : In 1955 you keep your promise. You take the train to Hamburg, for the first time in twenty-two years, to a city that is half ruins and half cranes, and you stand in front of a class of sixteen-year-olds at a Gymnasium in Eimsbüttel, and you tell them, in German, about Oradour-sur-Glane. They are very polite. They do not believe you. A boy at the back puts up his hand and says that his father was in the Das Reich division and that it was a battle against partisans. You go back every year until 1970. By the end, they believe you.
? testimony : The eleven pages you wrote on the churchyard wall at Oradour are read into the record at the military court in Bordeaux in 1953. A journalist from Le Monde asks you to give them to the Oradour memorial. You do.

Madeleine teaches at the girls' school on the rue Keller until 1965. She is a headmistress by then. She never wears blue again; she says it reminds her of the blackout paper on the windows. She dies in 1981, in her sleep, with her hand on your chest.

? !sarah_safe & !moise_safe & !parents_safe : Josette grows up. She becomes a nurse. She marries a doctor from Lyon and has three daughters. She is kind to you, always. She visits every Sunday. But there is a door in her that stays closed to you, all her life, and you know which morning it closed on. In 2005, when the Wall of Names is unveiled at the Shoah Memorial in the Marais, seventy-six thousand names of the Jews deported from France, she takes her granddaughters to find two of them, Lewin, Sarah, 1931, and Lewin, Moïse, 1939, and they touch the names with their fingers. Then she rings you, and you talk for an hour, for the first time in sixty years, about Sarah.

== ending_england
## Ending — The Other Shore
The fishing boat reaches the liner. Somebody hauls you up a rope ladder. The Arandora Star sails at midnight with three thousand Polish soldiers and two hundred civilians, and you stand at the rail at the stern and watch the lights of Saint-Jean-de-Luz go out one by one.

In England they intern you again, of course. You are a German. They take you off the ship at Liverpool. A week later the Arandora Star sails again, for Canada, carrying German and Italian internees, and is torpedoed off Ireland; eight hundred of them drown. You are not on it, by the luck of a list. Within a fortnight you are in a camp of tents on an unfinished housing estate at Huyton, near Liverpool, and then on the Isle of Man, behind barbed wire, in a seaside boarding house with the windows painted over, with a thousand other Germans and Austrians, many of them Jews, many of them men you knew from Les Milles. You laugh about it, the way Lindner taught you. Rats in a barrel, but an English barrel, with tea.

They let you out in 1941. You work in a bindery in Oxford, repairing the books of the Bodleian Library. You write to Madeleine through the Red Cross, twenty-five words at a time, once a month. Some of the letters arrive.

In 1945 you go back to Paris. Madeleine is there. Josette is there, twelve years old, and does not recognise you, and is polite to you, like a guest. Madeleine is kind. Madeleine has been alone for five years, through all of it, the hunger and the stars and the buses and the bells, and you were in Oxford, repairing books. You try. Both of you try, for three years. Then you go back to England, alone.

You live. That was the choice. You live, and you are safe, and you never forgive yourself for it, and you never know, as long as you live, whether you were right.

== ending_handed_over
## Ending — Surrender on Demand
"I would rather die," you say, "than go home to your Reich."

The German from Munich smiles. "That can be arranged," he says, quite pleasantly, and makes a cross on his list.

The French officer in the corner of the tent looks at the canvas wall. He does not say anything. He has his orders. Article Nineteen.

They hand you over at the demarcation line at Moulins in September 1940, with six other men from the camps of the south, in a lorry with a French driver and two French gendarmes who do not look at you, to two men in leather coats who do. You are taken to Paris, then Berlin, to the Gestapo headquarters on the Prinz-Albrecht-Strasse. Then to the concentration camp at Sachsenhausen, north of Berlin, where you are given a red triangle, for politicals, and a number.

You die there on the ninth of February 1942, of pneumonia and starvation and the beatings, in the infirmary block, aged thirty-six. Your number is entered in a ledger by a prisoner clerk with good handwriting. The camp administration sends a printed form to your last known address in Germany, informing your relatives of your death from heart failure and offering them your ashes for a fee of three Reichsmarks. Your last known address in Germany is your mother's flat in Hamburg. Your mother has been dead since 1938.

Madeleine never finds out what happened to you. In 1947 the Red Cross tells her you were "transferred to the German authorities in September 1940." That is all. She keeps writing letters until 1960.

You were brave. You told the truth. Nobody heard it but a man from Munich and a French officer looking at a wall.

== ending_deported
## Ending — Night and Fog
Stoltenberg looks at you across the desk with the geraniums and says, "Well, Herr Brandt. You're a stubborn Hamburger. I respect that. It won't help you, but I respect it."

You are not shot. You are sent to the transit camp at Compiègne, and in January 1944, in a cattle wagon, a hundred men to a wagon, to Germany. Nacht und Nebel, the order is called. Night and Fog: prisoners who disappear without trace, so that their families never know.

The train goes to the concentration camp at Neuengamme. Neuengamme is twenty kilometres from the centre of Hamburg. From the brickworks where the prisoners work, on clear days, you can see the spires of your city. The Michel church. The cranes of the harbour. It is a brickworks. Of course it is a brickworks. You laugh when you see it, the first day, standing in the snow in your striped clothes, and the Kapo beats you for laughing.

In April 1945, as the British army approaches, the SS evacuate the camp. Nine thousand prisoners are marched to the port of Lübeck and loaded on to ships, the Cap Arcona and the Thielbek, old liners, in the bay. On the third of May, five days before the end of the war, British aircraft, who do not know who is on board, bomb and sink them.

You are on the Cap Arcona. You are on the deck when the rockets hit. The water is very cold, the way the Allier was cold. You think of the Heine on your workbench with its split spine, that you never finished. You think of a drawing in coloured pencil. You are not afraid.

Seven thousand prisoners die in the bay of Lübeck that afternoon. Your body washes up on the beach at Neustadt in June, with hundreds of others. They are buried there in mass graves. In 1946 the French government writes to Madeleine to tell her you are "presumed dead in deportation."

Lucien comes to see her. He was never arrested. You never gave them his name.

== ending_shot_maquis
## Ending — The Beech Tree
They shoot you at dawn, against a beech tree, at the edge of the clearing, next to the boy from the SS.

It is not anyone's fault. Not exactly. It is the war. It is Oradour. It is Tulle. It is the rope still tied to the balconies, and the pram in the church, and twenty young men in a wood who have seen too much in a week and have nowhere to put it. You had a German accent, and German papers, and a book of drawings of guards and camps, and you were found on the road from Oradour the day after the massacre. You would have shot you too.

The girl who cooks takes your papers and the drawing from your breast pocket and gives them to the priest at Saint-Junien. The priest sends them to Paris in September, after the liberation, with a letter that says that a German civilian named Brandt was "executed in error by a unit of the FFI on the twelfth of June 1944 in the context of the events of Oradour," and that he is very sorry.

Madeleine has the drawing framed. It hangs in the workshop, over the bench, where the light is good, until she sells the workshop in 1960.

Josette visits the clearing in 1984, with her husband. There is nothing there. Just beech trees, and the wind.

## Ending — The Slow Kind
? in_mob : You die of the beating at the Bastille. Not that day. Four days later, in the bed in the flat on the rue de la Roquette, of the bleeding inside that nobody could see, on the thirtieth of August 1944, with the bells still ringing for the liberation and the flags still hanging in the street. A German, killed by Frenchmen, on the week Paris was freed. It is not a death that will be put on any memorial.

The hunger, the cold, the coughing that started in the brick dust and never went away, the winters without coal, the years of too little of everything. Your body simply runs out of the things it needs to go on. It happens to hundreds of thousands of people in Europe in these years, quietly, in beds and in queues and in camps, and nobody counts them as casualties of the war, although that is what they are.

== afterword
History note. Enemy Alien is fiction: Emil, Madeleine, the Lewins, Felix, Lindner, Kurt, Lucien, Hahn, Stoltenberg and everyone else in it are invented. But almost everything that happens to them happened to real people. The internment of German and Austrian refugees at Colombes and the Camp des Milles in 1939, the "ghost train" to Bayonne, Article 19 of the armistice, the exodus of June 1940, the roundup of Jewish men in the 11th arrondissement in August 1941, the shooting of hostages, the yellow star, the Vel d'Hiv roundup of 16-17 July 1942 and the separation of the children at Pithiviers and Beaune-la-Rolande, the bombing of Boulogne-Billancourt on 4 April 1943, the STO, the firestorm in Hamburg, the rescuers of Le Chambon-sur-Lignon, the hangings at Tulle and the massacre at Oradour-sur-Glane, the liberation of Paris, the shaving of women, the returning deportees at the Hotel Lutetia: all of these are real, and the numbers given in the story are the historical ones as far as they are known.

If you want to know more, the Camp des Milles memorial, the Mémorial de la Shoah in Paris, the Centre de la mémoire at Oradour-sur-Glane, and the Lieu de Mémoire at Le Chambon-sur-Lignon all tell these stories far better than a game can.
