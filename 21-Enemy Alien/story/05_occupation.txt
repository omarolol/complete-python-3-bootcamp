# PART FOUR - The Occupation, October 1940 - December 1941

== occupation
## Part Four — Berlin Time
Within a week you understand that you have come home to a city where you do not legally exist.

To eat, in occupied Paris, you need a ration card. To get a ration card you need an identity card. To get an identity card, as a foreigner, you need to present yourself at the Prefecture of Police, where, since the autumn, the files of all foreigners have been open to the German security services, who sit in an office on the third floor and drink coffee and read them. Your file says: Brandt, Emil. Born Hamburg 1905. Stateless (expatriated by the Reich, 1934). Social Democrat. Interned 1939. And in red ink: Suspect.

"Or," says Lucien, at the kitchen table, over a bottle of something brown he has brought that he says is calvados, "you could be somebody else."

He has a friend at the town hall of the twentieth arrondissement who has blank identity cards, real ones, with real stamps. With the right name, the right birthplace, you would not need to explain the accent. "Alsace," he says. "Half the Alsatians in Paris speak French like a cow chewing gravel. You'll be Émile Brun, of Colmar. Born 1905. Bookbinder. Married. Very boring. A hundred francs for my friend, for his trouble."

Madeleine looks at you. "If they catch you with false papers, it's prison. Or worse."

"If they read his real file," Lucien says, "it's worse anyway."

## The Census
On the twenty-seventh of September 1940 the German military command issues an ordinance: every Jew in the occupied zone must register at the police station of their district. Shortly afterwards, without even being asked, the Vichy government publishes its own law, the Statut des Juifs: Jews excluded from the civil service, from the army, from teaching, from the press, from the cinema.

The queues at the police station on the rue de l'Orillon go round the block in October. You see them every morning on your way to buy bread: old men in good coats with medals from 1914, women with prams, students, rabbis, families with children, all of them waiting patiently in the rain to put their names on a list for the French police, who have prepared the forms beautifully. There are separate files, colour-coded, by name, by nationality, by street, by profession. It is the best list of Jews anybody has ever made in France.

Isaac Lewin comes down to the workshop the night before he goes. He sits on the stool by the press, turning his hat in his hands.

"I'm going tomorrow," he says. "With Rivka. And the children too, they want the children on the list. You think I shouldn't."

== occ_winter
## The Winter of 1940
That winter is the coldest in Paris for fifty years. The Seine freezes at the edges. There is no coal. The Germans have taken the coal, and the trains to carry it, and the men to dig it are prisoners in Germany. In the flat, the water in the jug by the bed is frozen in the mornings, and Josette goes to school in two coats and gloves with the fingers cut off, and comes home with chilblains on her hands that crack and bleed.

There is no food. That is to say, there is food, for the Germans, and for those who can pay for the black market, and for those with relatives in the country. For everybody else there is a ration card, and a ration card in the winter of 1940 means about twelve hundred calories a day, and the shops do not always have enough to honour even that. Madeleine queues for three hours for four hundred grams of meat and gets to the front when there is none left. She queues for potatoes. There are no potatoes. There are swedes, rutabagas, the turnips that farmers used to feed to cattle, and Jerusalem artichokes, and Paris learns a hundred ways to cook them, and all of them taste of rutabaga.

People keep rabbits in their bathrooms. People keep hens on their balconies. The pigeons in the Luxembourg gardens disappear. So, in some quarters, do the cats.

In the workshop the glue freezes in the pot.

## Doctor Hahn
In March 1941 a German officer comes into the workshop.

You are at the bench, sewing sections, and the bell over the door rings, and you look up and there is a field-grey uniform, a Sonderführer's insignia, an officer's cap held politely in the hand, spectacles, a long sad clever face. He is about fifty. He is carrying a parcel wrapped in newspaper.

"I'm told you are the best binder in the eleventh arrondissement," he says in careful, perfect French. "A colleague recommended you. You rebound his Montaigne. Exquisite work. I have a Goethe."

He unwraps it on your bench. It is a first edition of the West-Eastern Divan, 1819, in its original boards, crushed at the corners, the spine split. You find that you have picked it up before you can stop yourself, and that you are turning it in your hands, looking at the sewing, and he is watching you with a small smile.

"You know what that is," he says. Then, in German: "You're from Hamburg, aren't you? Those vowels. My mother was from Altona."

? false_papers : "I am from Colmar, Herr Sonderführer," you say. "Alsace." He smiles more widely. "Of course you are," he says. "And I am from Timbuktu. Never mind. It's not my business. Books are my business."
? registered : "Brandt," he says. "Emil Brandt. Yes. I looked you up. I hope you don't mind. Expatriated in '34. Social Democrat. Interned by the French, poor man. Well. I'm not the Gestapo, Herr Brandt. I'm a librarian."
? no_papers : You do not answer. He looks at you for a long moment, and you can see him deciding not to ask. "Well," he says. "Never mind. It's not my business. Books are my business."

His name is Doctor Albrecht Hahn. Before the war he was a librarian at the State Library in Berlin. Now he works for an organisation called the Einsatzstab Reichsleiter Rosenberg, which collects books and documents in occupied countries "for research."

## August 1941
On the morning of the twentieth of August 1941 you wake at five to the sound of engines. Through the blacked-out window you see buses. Green and cream Paris buses, the ones from the TCRP, parked at the end of the street, ten of them, with their engines running, and French police in their capes everywhere, at every corner, blocking the ends of the rue de la Roquette, the boulevard Voltaire, the rue de Charonne.

It is the whole of the eleventh arrondissement. They close it off like a cork in a bottle. Nobody in, nobody out. Then they go building by building with their lists, the colour-coded lists from last October, looking for Jewish men between eighteen and fifty.

Isaac is forty-one.

He comes down the stairs at a quarter past five in his vest and his trousers with the braces hanging, barefoot, carrying his shoes, white as paper. Upstairs you can hear Rivka crying and the baby screaming.

"They're in the building opposite," he says. "Emil. Emil."

The workshop is downstairs. Behind the big standing press there is a space where you keep the boards, a cupboard with a false back, the size of a coffin, where in Hamburg you would have hidden a man without thinking. They are two doors away. You can hear them on the stairs of number 60.

## Hostages
The day after the roundup, the twenty-first of August, a young Communist called Pierre Georges, who will later be known as Colonel Fabien, shoots a German naval cadet in the back on the platform of the Barbès-Rochechouart Métro station. The cadet's name is Alfons Moser. He is twenty-one.

The Germans reply with posters.

The posters are yellow, with black borders, in German and French. They go up all over Paris, on walls, on Métro stations, on the columns where they used to advertise the theatre. AVIS. BEKANNTMACHUNG. They give the names of hostages who have been shot in reprisal. Communists, Jews, anybody already in prison. Three. Then ten. Then in October, after an officer is killed in Nantes, forty-eight in a single day, including a boy of seventeen called Guy Môquet, who writes a letter to his mother before they take him to the quarry.

On the poster in September, the third name is Marchal, Pierre, printer, born 1919. Lucien's cousin. They shoot him at the fortress of Mont-Valérien, in the clearing behind the chapel, at dawn.

Lucien comes to the workshop the next evening. He does not drink. He sits on Isaac's stool and he does not drink and you have never seen him not drink.

"It's started now," he says. "It's started properly." He looks at you. "I need people, Emil. People who can do things with paper. You know paper. Tracts. Cards. Stamps. You know how to make a thing look like what it isn't."

"What do you need?"

He tells you. Tracts first, to be left in Métro carriages and letterboxes and under the doors of churches. Then ration cards. Then, later, identity cards, with stamps cut by hand from rubber erasers and blocks of lino, for people who need to stop existing: escaped prisoners, young men avoiding the labour service, Jews, British airmen shot down over Belgium on their way south.

You are very good at stamps. You discover it the first night, cutting the eagle of the Prefecture backwards into a pink eraser with a scalpel by the light of a candle, while Madeleine sleeps.
