# PART SIX - The Vel d'Hiv roundup, July 1942

== rumor
## Part Six — Thursday the Sixteenth
It comes on Tuesday the fourteenth of July, the national holiday, which the Germans have forbidden anybody to celebrate. A warm grey day. No flags. The tricolour has been banned in the occupied zone, and so the women of the eleventh arrondissement go out walking in red, white and blue dresses, in threes, arm in arm, and the German soldiers don't understand.

That evening you hear it.

? spy : You hear it at the depot. Hahn's secretary, the girl from Leipzig, says it in the canteen to the fat corporal, not lowering her voice, because it never occurs to her that anybody there would care: the French police are going to do the big one on Thursday. Twenty-eight thousand names. Foreign Jews. Stateless. Everybody, this time, she says, women, children, the lot. They've requisitioned the buses and the Vélodrome d'Hiver. Thursday, four in the morning. You go to Lucien on your way home and tell him, and Lucien's face goes white, and he gets on his bicycle.

## The Sixteenth of July 1942
They come at ten past four. Not Germans. There is not one German in the rue de la Roquette that morning. French policemen, in their capes and their képis, in pairs, a sergeant and a constable, with lists, and French gendarmes in their helmets at the ends of the street, and the green and cream buses of the Paris transport company, with their drivers smoking at the wheel, waiting.

You hear them on the stairs of number 64. The heavy feet. The knock on the third floor. A man's voice, bored, saying "Police. Open up."

## The Vélodrome d'Hiver
Over two days the French police arrest thirteen thousand one hundred and fifty-two people in Paris. They had hoped for twenty-eight thousand, but many were warned and hid. Of those arrested, four thousand one hundred and fifteen are children.

The families with children are taken to the Vélodrome d'Hiver, the winter cycling stadium on the boulevard de Grenelle near the Eiffel Tower, where before the war there were six-day bicycle races and boxing matches and political rallies. They are kept there for five days. Eight thousand people. There is a glass roof, painted blue for the blackout, and July sun on it. There is no food except what the Quakers and the Red Cross are allowed to bring. There are eight toilets, and they are blocked by the first afternoon. There is no water except one hydrant. There are four doctors.

On the eighteenth you go there. You don't know why. You tell Madeleine you are going to work and you take the Métro to Grenelle and walk along the boulevard under the elevated railway, and there it is.

You hear it before you see it. Eight thousand people make a sound, even behind walls. It is not screaming. It is lower than that, a kind of roar, like the sea. And the smell, which reaches you a hundred metres away in the hot street.

There are French gendarmes all round it, in a cordon, keeping order.

## August 1942
In August you hear the rest of it from a nurse called Annette Monod, who works for the Red Cross and who has come back from Beaune-la-Rolande, and who sits in the workshop drinking chicory coffee with hands that will not stop shaking.

The families from the Vel d'Hiv were taken to the camps in the Loiret: Pithiviers and Beaune-la-Rolande, wooden barracks in the fields, behind barbed wire. There, in the first days of August, the parents were deported. To the East. To "labour in Poland." Without their children.

"They separated them," she says. "Gendarmes. French gendarmes. With hoses. With rifle butts. The mothers were holding on to them and the gendarmes were tearing their clothes off them to get the children loose. Three thousand children, Monsieur. Three thousand children, left on their own in the camp, the youngest two years old. They didn't know their own names. We tried to write their names on their clothes. They would swap clothes. They would lose their name tags. In the night they cried for their mothers, all of them at once."

Two weeks later, in the second half of August, the children were deported too, from Drancy, in convoys of a thousand, each with a few hundred adults mixed in, so that it would look like families. To the East.

"The Germans in Berlin wanted the children," she says. "Laval offered them. That's what they say. Laval said, take the children too."
