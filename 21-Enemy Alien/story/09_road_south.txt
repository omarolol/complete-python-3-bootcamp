# PART EIGHT - The road south, May - July 1944

== south
## Part Eight — The Plateau
In April 1944 Annette Monod comes back.

She is thinner and harder and she has cut her hair, and she sits in the kitchen without taking off her coat. "It's getting worse," she says. "Not better. Worse. They're losing, so they're hurrying. The Milice are going through the quarters house by house, with the Gestapo, looking for Jews, for réfractaires, for anybody. They're paying a hundred francs a head to informers. In March they took the children from the UGIF homes, the orphanages. Babies. They're taking babies out of orphanages, Monsieur Brandt."

She looks at you.

## The Train to Lyon
You leave from the Gare de Lyon on the eighth of May, at seven in the morning, in a third-class compartment, with a suitcase and a basket of food and the children.

? sarah_safe : Sarah, who is Suzanne Brun now, twelve years old, in a school coat and a beret, with her hair in two plaits, holding a book. She has been practising her Alsatian accent with you for a week, and it is very bad.
? moise_safe : Moïse, four, who has never been outside in his life that he can remember, and who stands at the window of the compartment with both hands pressed flat against the glass, staring at the world. At a cow. At a river. At a horse. "Mil," he whispers, "Mil, what's that?" A church. A bridge. Clouds.
? !sarah_safe & !moise_safe : Anna and Jakob Feldman, seven and five, who do not know you, who have been told by Annette that you are their uncle, and who sit side by side very straight, holding hands, and do not say one word all the way to Dijon.

The train is very slow. The Resistance has been blowing up the railway lines, and the Allied aircraft have been bombing the bridges and the marshalling yards, so that the train stops for hours in the middle of fields while the line is repaired ahead. At Laroche-Migennes the station has been bombed and you walk two kilometres past the wreckage to another train. At Dijon, a carriage of German soldiers going south is attached to the back.

At Chalon-sur-Saône, where the old demarcation line used to be, the train stops and there is a control.

A Feldgendarme, a German military policeman, with the metal crescent hanging on a chain round his neck that the soldiers call the dog collar, comes along the corridor with a French policeman behind him, checking papers.

== chambon
## Le Chambon-sur-Lignon
The little train from Saint-Étienne climbs up into the mountains for three hours, through pine forests and over gorges, past villages with grey stone churches, and comes out at last on to a high plateau of meadows and fir trees and wind, with the snow still lying in the shadows of the walls in May.

Le Chambon-sur-Lignon is a village of Protestants, the descendants of the Huguenots who were hunted by the King's dragoons for a hundred years. They remember. They have hidden people since the war began, in farms, in boarding houses, in children's homes, in the presbytery, in the forest: Jews, refugees, réfractaires, anybody who knocked on the door. Thousands. Everybody in the village knows. Nobody in the village talks.

You are met at the station by a woman of about sixty in a black dress and clogs, with a horse and cart, who calls herself Madame Héritier and doesn't ask your name. "They'll go to the farm at first," she says. "Then the school, when they're settled. They'll go to church on Sunday. They don't have to pray, but they have to sit in church. It looks better." She lifts the children into the cart. "They'll be all right with us. We've done this before. Two hundred and fifty years we've been doing this."

## The Sixth of June
You do not get home for two months.

On the sixth of June, as you are trying to get a train north from Saint-Étienne, the radio in the station café, turned up very loud, says that the Allies have landed in Normandy. The café goes silent. Then a man stands on a chair and starts to sing the Marseillaise, and a German soldier at the counter puts down his beer very carefully and walks out, and nobody stops singing.

On the seventh, every railway line in the centre of France is cut. The Resistance blows up everything that night: bridges, tunnels, junctions, signals, telegraph lines. The maquis come down out of the hills in the Limousin, in the Corrèze, in the Dordogne, with Sten guns dropped by English aircraft, and take over towns. They put up tricolours on the town halls. They think the liberation has come.

It has not. Not yet. The Germans have an armoured division in the south, near Montauban, the SS division Das Reich, fifteen thousand men with tanks, and it has been ordered north to Normandy, through the Limousin, and to clear the roads as it goes.

You go north by road. A lorry carrying potatoes to Clermont-Ferrand. A cart. Your feet. A gazogène van, the kind that runs on wood smoke, carrying wine. On the ninth you are in Tulle, in the Corrèze, and you arrive the morning after.

-> tulle

== tulle
In Tulle the day before, the maquis had attacked the German garrison and taken the town, and the Das Reich division had come and taken it back.

Then the Germans went from house to house and took all the men between sixteen and sixty, three thousand of them, and assembled them in the yard of the arms factory. They chose one hundred and twenty. Then they hanged them. From the balconies and the lamp posts and the trees along the avenue de la Gare, one after another, in the afternoon, with ropes from the factory, while the soldiers sat in the cafés and drank and played music on a gramophone. They ran out of rope after ninety-nine.

The bodies have been taken down by the time you arrive. You see the ropes. Some are still tied to the balconies, cut short, swinging a little in the wind. You see a woman scrubbing the pavement under one balcony with a brush and a bucket. She does not look up.

A hundred and forty-nine other men were sent to Dachau. Most of them will not come back.

You walk out of Tulle without stopping. Nobody stops in Tulle. Two days later, travelling north-west on a farm cart towards Limoges, you hear a name for the first time, from a farmer at a crossroads, who says it to the carter and then crosses himself. Oradour.

== oradour
## Oradour-sur-Glane
You reach it on the afternoon of the eleventh of June 1944, the day after.

It is a market town of about three hundred and fifty people, on a little river, among meadows, with a tramline to Limoges. It had a church, a school for girls and a school for boys, a post office, garages, cafés, a hotel, butchers, bakers, a cobbler, a doctor. On Saturday the tenth, a detachment of the Das Reich division drove into the town at two in the afternoon and told everybody to assemble on the fairground for an identity check. Everybody: the people of the town, the people from the farms around, the people who had come in for the tobacco ration, the children from the schools, the refugees from Lorraine and Spain, a few cyclists passing through.

They divided them. The men to the barns and garages. The women and children to the church.

In the barns, the men were shot with machine guns, in the legs first, and the barns set on fire with them inside.

In the church, the soldiers brought in a box which gave off a choking smoke. When the women and children tried to get out of the doors and windows, the soldiers shot them. Then they set the church on fire with them inside. Two hundred and forty-seven women. Two hundred and five children.

Six hundred and forty-three people in all. One woman got out of the church through a window behind the altar and lived. Five men got out of a barn.

Then the soldiers burned the town and drove away.

== oradour_carry
You carry the dead until it is dark. The men from Saint-Junien do not ask who you are. They give you a handkerchief soaked in vinegar to tie over your face. They give you gloves. == oradour_write
You sit on the wall of the churchyard and write. In the back of Lindner's sketchbook, the blank pages at the end, with a stub of pencil. Everything. The bell. The pram. The car. The tramline. The stove with a pot still on it. The old man with the bicycle and the little boy with red hair. The smell, which you try to describe and cannot. Twenty years later, those eleven pages will be read aloud by a schoolgirl at a memorial ceremony at Oradour, in French, and then by a schoolboy, in German.

== maquis
## The Maquis
They catch you the next morning on the road to Saint-Junien.

Six of them, very young, in berets and leather jackets and bits of uniform, with Sten guns and a hunting rifle and armbands that say FFI, the French Forces of the Interior, and the cross of Lorraine. They come out of a wood on both sides of the road, and you put up your hands, and one of them, a boy of about sixteen with a Sten gun, asks you where you are going, and you answer him, and he hears your accent.

After that it goes very fast.

They tie your hands behind your back with wire. They walk you into the wood, two kilometres, to a camp in a clearing: tents made of parachute silk, a fire, a dozen more young men, a girl cooking. They are all in a state you recognise. You saw it in Les Milles, in the weeks in May 1940. They have been to Oradour, or they know people who were. They are angry in a way that has nowhere to go.

They have another prisoner. He is sitting against a tree with his hands tied, in the field-grey uniform of the Waffen-SS, without a cap, with blood on his face. He is about seventeen.

The leader of the group is a man of about thirty, with a moustache and a policeman's tunic, who looks at your papers and your hand and your face. "German," he says. "Spy. You were at Oradour. Looking at what your friends did. Taking notes?" He holds up Lindner's sketchbook, which they took from your bag. "Drawings. Of what? Positions?"

"We shoot Germans," says the boy with the Sten gun. "After Oradour. That's the rule now."

== maquis_boy
The SS soldier under the tree has been listening to all of it. When the others go to eat, he says to you, very quietly, in German, "Please."

He has an accent you know. Alsatian. Not the Alsatian of a bookbinder from Colmar in a forged identity card, but the real thing, thick, from the villages.

His name is Joseph. He is seventeen. He is from a village near Sélestat. In February the Germans, who annexed Alsace in 1940, called up his year group into the Waffen-SS, all of them, by force. His father said if he ran they would send the whole family to a camp in Silesia. He has been a soldier for four months. He was not at Oradour, he says. His company was at Tulle. He says he didn't hang anybody. He says he stood guard. He says he only kept order.

He is crying. "They'll shoot me. They're going to shoot me tomorrow. They said. Please. Tell them. I'm French. I'm from Alsace. I'm French. I didn't want to. Tell them."
